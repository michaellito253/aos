US filing obligations while resident in the Netherlands

The United States taxes its citizens and green card holders on worldwide income, regardless of where they live. If you're a US person living in the Netherlands, this generally means you're still required to file a US federal tax return each year, even if you also file and pay tax in the Netherlands.

In most cases, mechanisms like the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion and the Foreign Tax Credit mean little or no US tax is actually owed — but the filing obligation itself doesn't go away, and missing it can create bigger problems later.

Netherlands‑specific tax mechanics

Dutch income tax is split into three "boxes," each taxed differently — and each one interacts with a US return in its own way:

Box 1

Work & home

Employment, self‑employment, and owner‑occupied home income, taxed at progressive rates. This is the box most closely mirrored by a US Form 1040.

Box 2

Substantial interest

Income from a 5%+ shareholding — relevant for Americans who own or co‑own a Dutch BV. Often triggers separate US foreign‑corporation reporting.

Box 3

Savings & investments

Taxes a deemed return on savings and investments rather than actual gains — a structural mismatch with US capital‑gains rules that needs to be reconciled every year.

A few situations come up often enough to call out on their own: relocating under the 30%‑ruling, owning a Dutch BV, and holding Dutch investment funds (which usually count as PFICs under US law and get taxed punitively if left unstructured).

Double taxation & the US–Netherlands tax treaty

The Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (Form 2555) and the Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) are the two primary tools that prevent the same income from being taxed twice. Which one applies — or whether both do, in combination — depends on your income type and situation.

The US–Netherlands tax treaty adds another layer, covering specific categories like pensions and social security. Dutch and US advisors sometimes give differing guidance here because they're each optimizing for one side of the picture — this is a common, and understandable, source of confusion.

FEIE · Form 2555

Usually simplest

Best for straightforward employment income where little or no US tax ends up owed anyway.

FTC · Form 1116

Usually more flexible

Better for higher earners, self‑employment, and investment income — credits carry over where the exclusion can't.

Reporting forms & Dutch banks

If the combined value of your non‑US financial accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point in the year, you likely need to file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114). This is also why Dutch banks ask US persons for their US tax details under FATCA — it's a reporting requirement on the bank's side, not a sign you've done something wrong.

Common scenarios

A quick read on how this typically plays out — click any card for the short version. For anything specific to your situation, a free assessment is the fastest way to get a straight answer.

Self‑employed / run a BV

+
Running a Dutch BV usually means US foreign corporation reporting (e.g. Form 5471) on top of your Dutch filings. Getting the entity structure right early avoids expensive fixes later.

Investments & crypto

+
Dutch Box 3 and US capital gains rules don't line up cleanly. Crypto and non‑US mutual funds (PFIC rules) are the two areas that most often catch people out.

Retirees & pensions

+
Dutch pensions (AOW, workplace pensions) are treated differently under US law than under Dutch law. The tax treaty usually resolves this, but it needs to be applied correctly.

Recently relocated

+
Your first year is the one to get right — split‑year treatment, the 30%‑ruling, and moving‑year income all interact in ways that are easy to misreport.

Never filed before

+
If you didn't know you had to file, the IRS Streamlined Procedure exists to bring you current penalty‑free in most cases — but it only works if you act before the IRS contacts you.

Accidental American in NL

+
Born a US citizen but never lived there? You likely still have a filing obligation. Options range from catching up to formally renouncing — worth understanding both before deciding.
