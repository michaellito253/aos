US tax preparation for Americans living in the UK

This guide is for US citizens, green card holders, and accidental Americans living in the United Kingdom. It covers your US filing obligations, how they interact with UK tax rules, and what to do next.

US filing obligations while resident in the UK

The United States taxes its citizens and green card holders on worldwide income, regardless of where they live. If you're a US person living in the UK, this generally means you're still required to file a US federal tax return each year, even if you also file a UK Self Assessment.

Because UK tax rates are often higher than US rates, the Foreign Tax Credit frequently eliminates most or all US tax owed in practice — but the filing obligation itself doesn't go away, and staying current matters more the longer you've been treating it as optional.

UK‑specific tax mechanics

Three areas of the UK tax and savings system most often catch long‑term US expats off guard:

Treaty

US–UK tax treaty

The treaty governs how income, pensions and gains are taxed across both countries — applied correctly, it's what makes double taxation avoidable rather than a given.

ISA / SIPP

ISAs & pensions

A Stocks & Shares ISA is tax‑free in the UK but not recognised as such by the IRS. SIPPs and workplace pensions carry their own US reporting requirements.

PFIC

UK‑domiciled funds

Many UK mutual funds and investment trusts are classed as PFICs for US purposes — a common, easy‑to‑miss source of unexpectedly punitive US tax treatment.

Note for review: the mechanics above are real, well‑known categories of US–UK tax interaction, but all specifics, thresholds and current guidance must be confirmed by AO's tax team before this page is published.

Double taxation & the US–UK tax treaty

The Foreign Tax Credit (Form 1116) is typically the primary tool for long‑term UK residents, since UK tax paid can usually be credited against US tax owed on the same income. The treaty adds specific provisions for pensions, social security and certain investment income.

US and UK advisors sometimes give differing guidance because each is optimizing for one side of the picture — worth flagging directly rather than leaving the visitor to reconcile it themselves.

FBAR / FATCA and UK bank accounts

If the combined value of your non‑US financial accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point in the year, you likely need to file an FBAR (FinCEN Form 114). This is also why UK banks and investment platforms ask US persons for their US tax details under FATCA — a reporting requirement on the institution's side, not a sign of a problem.
